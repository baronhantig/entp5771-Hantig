Peer Review Given — Scoop Demand Instrument
Part 1: Checklist
1. Are the six sections present and in order?
No. The instrument contains six labeled sections, but they are not in the required order. The correct order is screen, context, describe, appeal, valuation, and characteristics. In this instrument, Section 3 is “How much would you pay?”, while Section 4 is “The product” and Section 5 is “What do you think?” This means respondents answer the valuation questions before seeing the product description or rating its appeal. This is a major problem because respondents are being asked to value something they do not yet understand, and the early price questions may make the rest of the survey feel like a negotiation.
2. Does the screener exclude based on the customer’s purchase decision, and is there an age gate?
No. Q1, “Do you own a dog?”, is connected to the customer group, although it may exclude people who regularly purchase supplies for a dog they care for but do not personally own. Q2, “Would you consider investing in a sustainable pet care product?”, screens for the wrong decision. Customers are deciding whether to purchase dog-waste bags, not whether to invest in the company. The wording may also make respondents think they are being asked to provide money to a startup.
The instrument also does not have the required age gate. “Age” appears as Q10 in the final characteristics section, but by then the respondent has already completed the survey. The first question must ask whether the respondent is 18 or older, and answering “no” must end the survey immediately. This is a serious problem, although it would be simple to fix before fielding.
3. Does the context section oversell or promise that the product will exist?
Yes. The statements “We’re launching Scoop” and “before we go to market next spring” promise that the product will exist. The phrase “the world’s first truly compostable dog waste system” is also promotional and makes an unsupported superiority claim. This wording could make respondents feel that they are evaluating an established launch rather than providing evidence that will help determine whether the product should move forward.
A more neutral context statement would explain that the answers will help determine whether the product should be offered and how it might be priced, without promising that it will launch.
4. Could I judge the product’s value from the description alone?
Not completely. The description says that Scoop includes “compostable dog waste bags on a refill roll, with a clip-on dispenser that attaches to a leash,” but important details are missing. I would need to know how many bags come on one roll, how many rolls are in a monthly refill pack, whether the dispenser is included, and exactly what unit I am being asked to price.
The instrument refers to “one refill roll,” “Scoop refills,” a “monthly refill pack,” and a “three-month pack.” These may represent different quantities. Without a consistent unit, I would have to guess what I was buying.
5. Is there marketing copy that survived from the campaign page?
Yes. The description contains several promotional claims:
“a revolutionary approach”
“a problem every dog owner hates”
“no more guilt, no more plastic”
“never jams”
“Once you’ve tried Scoop, you’ll never go back”
“the world’s first truly compostable dog waste system”
These statements tell respondents how they are supposed to feel about the product. They should be replaced with factual details about the materials, bag quantity, dispenser, disposal requirements, and what is included in the unit being evaluated.
6. Is the elicitation right for the product?
Yes, the author’s choice of how-many demand makes sense because dog-waste bags are purchased and used repeatedly. A customer may need several refill rolls within a month, unlike a product such as a wallet or mattress that would usually be purchased once during the decision period.
However, the instrument does not define a consistent unit or period. The product is described as being “sold as a monthly refill pack,” while Q3 asks about “one refill roll.” Before the how-many elicitation can work, the author must decide whether the unit is one roll or one monthly pack and state the period clearly.
7. Is the valuation wording exact?
No. The three anchors are present, but they are in the wrong order. The instrument asks:
“What is the most you would ever pay for one refill roll?”
“At that price, how many would you take?”
“If Scoop refills were permanently free, how many would you take?”
The free anchor must come first, followed by the maximum price, and then the quantity at that price. The current sequence does not create the intended demand line.
The questions should be reordered and rewritten as:
If one Scoop refill roll were permanently free, how many refill rolls would you take per month?
What is the most you would ever pay for one Scoop refill roll?
At that price, how many Scoop refill rolls would you take per month?
8. Are the unit and period stated inside the question itself?
No. Q3 includes a possible unit in “one refill roll,” but it does not include a decision period. Q4 asks, “At that price, how many would you take?” without stating either the unit or the period. Q5 asks, “If Scoop refills were permanently free, how many would you take?” but it also leaves out the period and does not clearly define whether “refills” means rolls or monthly packs.
The period must appear in the questions themselves. For example, the author could ask how many refill rolls the respondent would take per month.
9. Can a respondent easily answer zero?
No. Q4 and Q5 use the dropdown “1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6+.” A respondent cannot select zero. Someone who would not take the product, even if it were free, is forced to choose at least one. This would artificially increase the estimated demand.
Both quantity questions need to permit 0. The author should use a numerical response field that accepts zero or add zero as the first dropdown option. A numerical field would be better because “6+” also combines several different quantities into one answer.
Part 2: Respondent Experience
I worked through the full eligible path as a simulated respondent. I selected “yes” for “Do you own a dog?” so that I could test the rest of the instrument. I hesitated at “Would you consider investing in a sustainable pet care product?” because I read “investing” as providing money to a business rather than buying dog-waste bags. I would normally answer “no” to an investment question, which would end the survey even though I could still be a possible customer. To continue testing the form, I had to answer in the way I thought the author wanted.
I had to guess when answering “What is the most you would ever pay for one refill roll?” because I did not yet know how many bags were on a roll, whether the dispenser was included, or how long one roll would last. I also did not know whether I was pricing one roll, one monthly pack, or part of the advertised three-month pack.
For “At that price, how many would you take?” and “If Scoop refills were permanently free, how many would you take?”, I did not know whether the author meant per month, per year, or in one purchase. The dropdown also prevented me from entering zero and limited larger answers to “6+.”
The product description came too late because I had already provided my price answers. Even after reading it, I still did not know the number of bags or rolls included. The description also made me feel pushed toward a positive response through claims such as “no more guilt” and “you’ll never go back.”
The characteristics section felt unnecessarily long. Questions such as “ZIP code,” “What breed?”, and “Would you recommend Scoop to a friend?” do not have an obvious connection to the stated demand analysis. The age question also appeared too late to function as a gate. I could not evaluate whether the product image loaded correctly because the submitted instrument only included the placeholder “[image: product photo]” rather than the actual form image.
Part 3: The One Thing
If the author changes only one thing, the author should rebuild the entire valuation block using a single, clearly defined unit and period. The three questions should appear after the neutral product description and appeal section, follow the required order, and allow zero. I recommend using one refill roll as the unit and one month as the period:
If one Scoop refill roll were permanently free, how many refill rolls would you take per month?
What is the most you would ever pay for one Scoop refill roll?
At that price, how many Scoop refill rolls would you take per month?
This is the most important change because incorrect valuation data cannot be repaired after respondents complete the survey.
Part 4: Would I Field This?
No, I would not field this instrument in its current form. The how-many elicitation is appropriate, but the valuation questions are asked before respondents understand the product, the three anchors are in the wrong order, the unit and period are unclear, and the quantity fields do not allow zero. The missing age gate and the screener’s focus on “investing” are also serious problems. These issues would create biased or uninterpretable demand data, so the instrument needs to be revised and tested again before it is given to respondents.
