# Profit Model on Supplied Data

## Part 1 — The curve and the reading

The model comes out robust, with a pretty wide range of prices where the business could make money. I used the mid estimates of $0.78 per cola, $14,000 in monthly commitments, and 16,500 people reached. I scaled the Week 5 demand curve by 16,500 divided by 46, including the zero responses, and subtracted the costs from monthly revenue. The price range is $0–$3.51.

| Result | Mid-estimate model |
| :--- | :--- |
| Peak | $1.95 per cola, with $198,843.30 in monthly profit |
| Positive-profit band | Above about $0.8167 through $3.51; one continuous band |
| Width | About $2.6933, or 76.73% of the price range |
| Reading | robust |

If prices are charged in whole cents, profit starts at $0.82. That leaves a lot of room to adjust the price, as long as people actually buy close to what they reported.

## Part 2 — Sensitivity

I changed one input at a time and kept the others at the mid estimates. For the quantity anchors, I lowered both reported quantities by the same percentage for every respondent, without changing the maximum price they would pay.

| Actionability | Input | Becomes fragile | Becomes impossible |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Testable | Quantity anchors | Below about 9.09% of the original quantities | At about 6.58% or less |
| Testable | Reachable population | Below about 1,499.07 people | At about 1,085.31 people or fewer |
| Designable | Unit cost | Above about $2.3048 per cola | At about $2.8595 or more |
| Deferrable | Monthly commitment | Above about $154,095.82 | At about $212,843.30 or more |

The thresholds are rounded. For whole-person population counts, 1,500 or more is robust, 1,086–1,499 is fragile, and 1,085 or fewer is impossible. The reading changes to fragile when the profitable band falls below 28% of the price range, or $0.9828. Some cases have two separate profitable bands. I added their widths without counting the gap where the business loses money.

The supplied low and high estimates all stayed robust when I changed one input at a time. I had to go beyond those ranges to find where the reading changed.

| Input varied | Band share at the supplied low estimate | Band share at the supplied high estimate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Unit cost: $0.65 to $0.95 | 80.50% | 71.82% |
| Monthly commitment: $10,000 to $20,000 | 77.03% | 76.28% |
| Reachable population: 8,000 to 25,000 | 75.59% | 77.09% |

Quantities and reach are testable through a small sales test and checking gym membership numbers. I could change the packaging or production setup to lower unit cost, and I could put off monthly commitments until I know more. None of these inputs is completely fixed. The thresholds tell me what would change the decision, not how likely those changes are.

## Part 3 — Read the peak sceptically

Looking at prices one cent apart between $1.70 and $2.20, the largest drop near the $1.95 peak was:

| Price | Monthly profit |
| :--- | :--- |
| $2.00 | $169,350.18 |
| $2.01 | $141,703.22 |

That is a $27,646.97 drop from raising the price by one cent. Two respondents said $2.00 was their maximum price and reported buying 50 and 15 colas a month at that price. At $2.01, both drop out. Scaling those answers to 16,500 people makes the drop much bigger. The model assumes they stop buying completely once the price passes their maximum, but real customers might not react that suddenly. Across the full price range, there is an even larger drop at $2.50–$2.51, from $143,350.28 to $100,497.04.

I would not settle on $1.95 just because it has the highest profit in the model. I would try nearby prices, like $1.90 and $1.95, and compare actual sales. Since the profitable band is wide, I do not need to get the price exactly right on the first try.

## Part 4 — What I would do

I would move ahead with a small sales test, but I would hold off on the full monthly commitment. The robust reading and wide profitable band make the idea worth trying. First, I would sell a small batch at gyms and track how much people actually buy. The model becomes fragile below about 9.09% of the reported quantities and impossible at about 6.58%, so purchases would have to fall a lot to change the answer. Still, I want to see people paying and coming back before committing. I would also check how many gym members I can reach. These tests change one input at a time, so if both purchases and reach fall short, I would have less room for error.
