# Scale

For this analysis, I am using one Bare Bones Wallet 2 at its $60 MSRP as one unit and a 12-month decision period. My demand survey is still collecting, so this analysis focuses on what I can currently defend about reach and the amount of contribution available from each sale.

## 1. Market Size Is Not Demand

The possible market for wallets is much larger than the group I can actually reach through this project. A national market-size estimate could show that many people buy wallets, but it would not tell me how many people I can reach, how many would consider this particular wallet, or how many would buy it for $60 during the next 12 months.

For that reason, I do not use a national wallet-market estimate as my reachable population, `N`. It would be a ceiling rather than evidence of demand.

## 2. Existing Is Not Being Reachable

My two recruiting channels were my University of Utah fraternity and Reddit's r/SampleSize.

My fraternity is the clearest example of a reachable population because I am a member, attend the meetings, and have direct access to approximately 120 members. During the first recorded recruiting period, the survey was sent or presented to 124 recipients. Nine started it, six passed the screener, and six finished.

That produces an observed start rate of:

**9 ÷ 124 = 7.3%**

and a completed-response rate of:

**6 ÷ 124 = 4.8%**

This matters because having access to 124 people did not mean that 124 people were willing to engage with the product or survey.

My second channel was r/SampleSize. The post received 247 views, but only two people started, passed the screener, and finished. Relative to the recorded post views, the completed-response rate was:

**2 ÷ 247 = 0.8%**

I do not treat the 247 Reddit views as a population of 247 reachable wallet buyers. A post view does not tell me whether the viewer was an adult who chooses a wallet, carries physical cards, or was even part of the customer group. I also cannot repeatedly identify or contact those 247 viewers.

The Reddit result is still useful because it gives evidence about the difficulty of getting attention outside a group where I already have direct access and credibility. The online channel generated much less engagement than the fraternity channel.

There are also structural limits on reach. The fraternity channel is easy for me to access, but it is small and concentrated among college-aged men at one university. Reddit provides access beyond the University of Utah, but I do not control distribution on the platform and have less trust or direct contact with the people who see the post. For a real product launch, getting someone to become a paying customer would probably be harder than getting someone to complete a free three-minute survey.

## 3. Sample Is Not Population

For my defensible reachable population, I use the fraternity population rather than a national wallet market or the number of Reddit post views.

**Reachable population (`N`) = 124**

For the first recorded fraternity sample:

**Usable responses (`n`) = 6**

Therefore:

**Multiplier = N ÷ n**

**124 ÷ 6 = 20.7**

My multiplier is approximately **20.7**.

The assumption behind this multiplier is important. Scaling the six usable responses to the 124-person reachable population assumes that the fraternity members who responded behave like the members who did not respond. I do not have evidence that this is true.

In fact, nonresponse may make that assumption especially risky. People who chose to complete a survey about a wallet may have been more interested in wallets or in the product than people who ignored the request. With only six usable fraternity responses, one person's answer also represents a large share of the sample.

The two completed Reddit responses give me some evidence outside the fraternity, but they do not justify expanding `N` to Reddit's entire membership, all University of Utah students, or the U.S. wallet market. I did not sample those populations in a way that supports that extrapolation.

Using `N = 124` makes the scale of this experiment small, but it keeps the model internally consistent with the population I actually reached.

## 4. Adoption Is Not Penetration

My survey asks respondents for the maximum amount they would pay for one bare-bones modular card wallet during the next 12 months. A respondent whose stated willingness to pay is at least $60 can eventually be counted as willing at the $60 benchmark when I analyze the demand data.

That still does not mean the person will actually buy.

Stated willingness to pay is evidence about demand, while penetration is the share of the reachable population that actually has to become buyers during the period I am modeling. Someone can like the product or say that $60 is acceptable without completing a real purchase.

## 5. Contribution Per Sale

From my cost-structure analysis, estimated variable cost per wallet ranges from:

**Low variable cost: $15.43**

**High variable cost: $24.59**

At the $60 benchmark price:

**Low-cost contribution = $60 − $15.43 = $44.57 per wallet**

**High-cost contribution = $60 − $24.59 = $35.41 per wallet**

This means each $60 sale contributes approximately **$35.41–$44.57** toward fixed commitments after the costs triggered by that sale.

## 6. Required Sales and Penetration

The required-sales equation is:

**q required = f ÷ (p − c)**

The problem in applying this equation to Ohsnap is that public information does not establish an incremental fixed commitment, `f`, for selling Wallet 2 beyond the Kickstarter campaign.

I identified a comparable 300-unit minimum production order in my cost-structure research, but I do not use the entire purchase price of those wallets as `f` here. Manufacturing cost is already included in variable cost `c`. Treating the cost of 300 wallets as fixed cost and then subtracting manufacturing again from each sale would count the same cost twice.

The 300-unit MOQ still matters because it would require an upfront inventory commitment before the company knows how many customers will buy. It creates cash and unsold-inventory risk, but it does not create a second manufacturing expense in this break-even equation.

Because I do not have evidence of Ohsnap's actual incremental post-Kickstarter fixed cost, I cannot claim one exact required-sales number. Instead, I calculated several fixed-commitment scenarios to show how the required number of buyers changes as `f` changes.

| Hypothetical Fixed Commitment | Required Sales at $44.57 Contribution | Required Sales at $35.41 Contribution | Required Penetration of N = 124 |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: |
| $500 | 12 wallets | 15 wallets | 9.7%–12.1% |
| $1,000 | 23 wallets | 29 wallets | 18.5%–23.4% |
| $2,000 | 45 wallets | 57 wallets | 36.3%–46.0% |
| $3,000 | 68 wallets | 85 wallets | 54.8%–68.5% |

These are scenario calculations, not claims that Ohsnap actually has $500, $1,000, $2,000, or $3,000 of incremental fixed cost. Their purpose is to show how much the unknown fixed commitment matters to the decision.

Because my survey asks about one wallet during the next 12 months, I do not need to divide required unit sales by multiple units per buyer. One required sale corresponds to one required buyer.

## 7. Is the Required Penetration Plausible?

The answer depends heavily on the fixed commitment, and my current reach evidence makes even the lower scenarios worth treating cautiously.

For example, a $1,000 fixed commitment would require approximately 23–29 buyers, or about **18.5%–23.4% of my reachable population of 124**. That is not the same as saying I "only need about 20%." I would have to convert roughly one out of every four or five people in a population consisting primarily of fraternity members at one university.

My actual funnel has not demonstrated anything close to that level of conversion. Only 6 of 124 fraternity recipients completed the survey, a **4.8% completion rate**, and completing a free survey requires much less commitment than spending $60 on a wallet. The Reddit channel was even weaker, with two completions from 247 recorded post views.

At a $2,000 fixed commitment, required penetration rises to approximately **36.3%–46.0%**. At $3,000 it rises to approximately **54.8%–68.5%**. Those scenarios look especially difficult relative to the reach I have demonstrated so far.

The uncertainty in variable cost matters, but the result does not completely flip between the low and high ends. At every fixed-cost scenario, the high variable-cost case requires more buyers. The larger unresolved issue is the actual fixed commitment. Obtaining Ohsnap's true incremental production or tooling commitment would improve this model more than making the payment-processing or shipping estimates slightly more precise.

The survey is still collecting and will continue beyond the deadline for this assignment, so I do not yet have a final willingness-to-pay curve. For this week's analysis, I use the recruiting funnel collected so far as my evidence about reach and the $60 MSRP as the benchmark price being tested. The cost analysis tells me how many buyers different levels of fixed commitment would require. The demand data being collected separately will eventually provide evidence about how many potential customers are actually willing to pay that price.
