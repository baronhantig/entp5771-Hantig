# Milestone 2 — Next Question

## Original Week 3 decision question

Would at least 30% of qualified potential customers unfamiliar with Ohsnap be willing to purchase one Bare Bones Wallet 2 for $60, providing enough evidence to justify producing it for sale beyond the Kickstarter campaign?

My Week 3 stopping rule was to collect at least 50 qualified responses and check whether at least 30% indicated they would purchase at $60.

## Answer from the evidence

**Not yet:** only 8 of 39 qualified survey respondents (20.5%) reported willingness to pay $60, below my 30% threshold, and I did not reach the planned sample of 50 qualified respondents.

This changes my decision about committing to broader production at the $60 retail price: I would not make that commitment based on the evidence collected. My confidence in that decision is limited because the sample was smaller than planned, respondents came through two different recruiting channels, and stated willingness to pay is not an actual purchase. The result does not establish that nobody would buy at $60; it shows that my precommitted evidence standard was not met.

## What the profit analysis adds

The modeled profit results suggest that a lower price could work under favorable assumptions, but the outcome depends strongly on costs and the number of actual buyers. Using a reachable population of 124 and the survey's stated willingness-to-pay curve, the verified raw-response model peaks at **$45 under low variable costs and $55 under high variable costs**. Under the hypothetical $500 fixed-cost commitment, the modeled price range with positive profit was substantial; under $3,000 in fixed costs, no modeled price was profitable. These are scenarios, not verified Ohsnap operating results. In particular, the model assumes survey willingness to pay translates into purchases, and the 124-person fraternity population may not be represented by the pooled survey sample.

## Next question to answer

**If I offer the Bare Bones Ohsnap Wallet 2 to qualified potential customers outside the Kickstarter audience at a clearly stated test price in the **$45–$55** range, what proportion will make a real purchase or meaningful purchase commitment, and is that conversion sufficient to cover verified per-wallet and customer-acquisition costs?**

I could test this with a clearly priced purchase offer or a permitted reservation/preorder experiment, tracking the number of qualified people reached, the number who take the purchase action, and the effort or dollars spent to reach them. Before using the result to justify production, I would also need firmer supplier, fulfillment, and fixed-cost estimates. I would not treat another free willingness-to-pay survey as equivalent to actual conversion.

## My call

I would not commit to broader production yet. My original $60 decision threshold was not met, and I fell short of my planned sample size. The profit model gives me a reason to investigate a lower price rather than abandon the idea outright, but its favorable result is not robust to every plausible fixed-cost assumption, and it has not been tested against actual paying-customer behavior. The next useful investment is a limited, measurable purchase-conversion test and verification of real costs—not a full production commitment. If real conversion and credible costs support a positive margin after acquisition effort, I would reconsider the decision; until then, I would keep the commitment small.
