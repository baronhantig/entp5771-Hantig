# Competitive advantage — Ohsnap Wallet 2 vs. Secrid Cardprotector

## What the estimates can and cannot show

My product is **Ohsnap Wallet 2 (firm i)**; its rival is **Secrid Cardprotector (firm j)**. I use the two screened-in survey responses and the descriptive additive fit reported in `system.md`. The sample is too small to measure a reliable competitive position: one respondent had $0 willingness to pay for both wallets, and the other violated both rival-price monotonicity comparisons. The two estimated cross-price coefficients are negative and **cannot be interpreted as substitution**. The numbers below are arithmetic comparisons, not validated market advantages.

## Week 11 prediction checked

In `rival-profile.md`, I predicted **d_i < d_j**, with about **60% confidence**: Secrid's established brand, familiar mechanism, and lower listed price might make Ohsnap customers more willing to switch toward Secrid than the reverse. The descriptive fit gives **d_i = -0.0125** and **d_j = -0.0200**, so **d_i > d_j numerically**, contrary to the predicted ordering. But both coefficients have the wrong sign for substitutes. I cannot say the survey disproved my switching-cost reasoning. I may have overestimated Secrid's advantage, but the responses could also reflect unclear product descriptions, confusing scenarios, or respondents who did not view the wallets as substitutes. I cannot distinguish whether the error concerns Ohsnap customers or Secrid customers with these data.

The d comparison is unusable. **Cost is the only dimension for which I have external estimated ranges**, and those ranges do not establish which firm actually has the lower unit cost. Ohsnap's $60 MSRP and Secrid's approximately $49 price are retail prices, not unit costs.

## Durability and what to do next

The chapter's ranking matters more than these unstable numerical comparisons. **A lower unit cost is usually visible and relatively easy for a competent rival to copy** through suppliers, materials, or production scale. An advantage in **low own-price sensitivity (low b)** would be hardest to erode because trust, fit, habit, and switching costs develop over time. **Asymmetric substitution (d_i versus d_j)** falls between those: it may reflect real switching friction, but it also depends on a rival's prices and product choices. A small apparent appeal lead that a rival could undo within a quarter would be a head start, not a durable position. At present, I cannot claim Ski Pal-style strategic durability for either wallet from this survey; specifically, I cannot claim Ohsnap has durable loyalty or favorable switching asymmetry. The next useful steps are to show clear pictures and comparable features for both wallets, recruit a substantially larger sample, rerun the four price conditions, and seek better unit-cost evidence. Until then, **no measured durable competitive advantage has been established**.
