1. What gets committed, and when.
 The commitment is to move forward with producing Ohsnap Wallet 2 for sale beyond the Kickstarter campaign. The campaign ends on September 26, 2026, and the estimated delivery date is February 2027, so the production commitment would need to occur after the campaign closes and before fulfillment begins.
2. Who the customer is.
 The customer is a person who currently carries a wallet, regularly carries only a small number of cards, and would prefer a slimmer way to carry and quickly access those cards. This is a customer I could realistically find and survey this semester without limiting the sample to people who already know or use Ohsnap.
3. What one unit is.
 One unit is one Bare Bones Ohsnap Wallet 2 at its regular retail price of $60. The Kickstarter campaign lists this version at a $60 MSRP and a discounted Kickstarter price of $45. Using the base version keeps the purchase decision focused on the wallet itself rather than adding MagSafe or premium-material features.
4. Over what period a buyer decides again.
 A wallet is an infrequent replacement purchase rather than something a customer buys weekly or monthly. For this project, I would define the purchase period as one wallet-replacement decision cycle, meaning the customer considers buying again when the current wallet needs to be replaced or when the customer decides to switch to a different wallet.
5. Which unknown is blocking me — and is it demand?
 The blocking unknown is whether customers outside Ohsnap’s existing Kickstarter audience would be willing to purchase Wallet 2 at its $60 retail price. The Kickstarter campaign had already attracted 2,211 backers and $190,001 in pledges when the campaign information was captured, so there is already strong evidence that some customers are interested in the product. What remains uncertain is whether that demand extends beyond early Kickstarter backers and remains strong at the full retail price.
This unknown is blocking because weak demand at $60 would make a broader production commitment much riskier. It is also learnable because I can survey potential customers who are unfamiliar with Ohsnap and measure their willingness to purchase at that price. I considered manufacturing feasibility as another possible unknown, but rejected it because I do not have access to Ohsnap’s supplier contracts, manufacturing costs, or internal production data, so it would be difficult to learn through the survey-based experiment required in this course.
Part 2 — The Question
Decision question:
 Would at least 30% of qualified potential customers unfamiliar with Ohsnap be willing to purchase one Bare Bones Wallet 2 for $60, providing enough evidence to justify producing it for sale beyond the Kickstarter campaign?
Favorable answer:
 If at least 30% of qualified respondents indicate that they would purchase the wallet at $60, I would move forward with the broader production commitment because the result would suggest that demand exists beyond the discounted Kickstarter audience.
Unfavorable answer:
 If fewer than 30% of qualified respondents indicate that they would purchase the wallet at $60, I would not make the same production commitment. I would reconsider the price, product configuration, or scale of production before moving forward.
Part 3 — The Stopping Rule
I will have enough evidence when I have responses from at least 50 qualified potential customers and can determine whether at least 30% would purchase one Bare Bones Wallet 2 at $60.
One note: the 30% threshold and 50-person sample are judgment calls, not numbers supported by the Kickstarter file. The professor explicitly says the stopping rule may feel arbitrary at this stage, so this is defensible as long as Baron commits to it before collecting data.
